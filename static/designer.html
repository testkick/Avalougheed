The Designer

Ava Lougheed

Shoe designer · Milan-trained · based in Vancouver

I don't believe in shoes that whisper. I design for people who want their footprints to leave a story behind.

Beginnings

Milan taught me how a shoe is built. Vancouver taught me what to put inside it.

I trained at the Arsutoria School in Milan — one of the few places in the world that still teaches shoemaking the way it was done a hundred years ago. For a year, I spent my days bent over a lasting bench with Italian craftsmen who treated a pattern as something closer to music than a schematic. I learned to cut uppers by hand. I learned how a sole should flex, and where. I learned that there are twelve different ways to stitch the same seam, and that eleven of them are wrong for any given shoe.

What I didn't expect to learn was patience. Shoes made the traditional way take time to tell you what they want to be. You keep adjusting. You keep walking in them. You keep listening.

When I came home to Vancouver, I had the technique. What I was still finding was the voice — the reason to make shoes at all, in a world that already has too many of them.

A Method

I design by walking.

My sketchbooks aren't full of shoes. They're full of other things — the curve of a railing at the Duomo, the exact pink of a flower stall in Testaccio at six in the morning, the way afternoon light falls through the trees at Trout Lake. Shoes come later. First I collect the moments.

I think the best shoes are the ones that remember a place for you. You pull them on and you feel a little more like the version of yourself who was walking somewhere beautiful. That's the whole thesis.

I don't chase trends. Trends are consensus, and consensus is where personality goes to die. I'd rather make something you'll still reach for in five years because it fits who you are.

The Vision

This first shoe is a thesis. The brand is the body of work.

The 300 pairs we're releasing this fall are the opening sentence. They tell you how I think — the leather, the stitching, the colour, the refusal to be quiet. But they're not the whole story.

Over the next two years I'm building a small, deliberate collection: a loafer, an ankle boot, a sandal, and a second colourway of the original flat. Four shoes, each one earning its place. Each made in editions of a few hundred, in the same small factory in Italy, with the same standard applied: a shoe you'll want to keep, not replace.

Beyond the collection itself, the brand is a point of view. It's for the person who treats getting dressed as a form of self-respect. The one who'd rather be a little too much than a little forgettable. The one who thinks "fearlessly fabulous" isn't a slogan — it's a way of showing up.

That's the audience, and that's the plan: build slowly, make things that last, and give that person something new to look forward to every season.
